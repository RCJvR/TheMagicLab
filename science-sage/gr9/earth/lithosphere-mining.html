The Lithosphere and Mining of Mineral Resources

Rock never stops changing. Follow it through the rock cycle, then discover how South Africa mines its mineral wealth — and at what cost.

What is the lithosphere?

The lithosphere is the solid, rocky outer layer of the Earth. It consists of the Earth's crust (the thin outermost layer) and the rigid uppermost part of the mantle below it. The lithosphere is broken into large sections called tectonic plates, which move very slowly over time.

The lithosphere is the source of all the rocks, minerals, and soil that support life, agriculture, construction, and industry on Earth.

The three main rock types

All rocks on Earth fall into one of three categories, based on how they were formed:

Rock typeHow it formsExample
IgneousForms when magma (molten rock underground) or lava (molten rock on the surface) cools and solidifiesGranite, basalt
SedimentaryForms when small particles of sediment (sand, mud, shell fragments) are compacted and cemented together over timeSandstone, limestone, shale
MetamorphicForms when existing rock is changed by intense heat and/or pressure, without melting completelyMarble (from limestone), slate (from shale)

The rock cycle

Rocks are not permanent — they are continually being transformed from one type into another over long periods of geological time, in a continuous loop called the rock cycle:

  • Magma/lava cools and solidifies → forms igneous rock.
  • Weathering and erosion break igneous (or any) rock down into sediment (small particles).
  • Compaction and cementation of sediment over time → forms sedimentary rock.
  • Heat and pressure (e.g. deep underground, or near tectonic activity) transform existing rock without fully melting it → forms metamorphic rock.
  • If any rock is heated enough to fully melt, it becomes magma again, and the cycle repeats.
Key idea: The rock cycle has no fixed starting or ending point — any rock type can eventually be transformed into any other rock type, given enough time, heat, pressure, weathering or melting.

South Africa's mineral wealth

South Africa is one of the world's richest countries in mineral resources, and mining has played a central role in its economic history and development.

MineralSignificance
GoldSouth Africa was historically the world's largest gold producer, especially from the Witwatersrand Basin (Johannesburg area)
DiamondsMajor deposits found near Kimberley and along the West Coast; South Africa remains a significant global producer
Platinum group metalsSouth Africa holds the majority of the world's known platinum reserves, mainly in the Bushveld Complex
CoalAbundant in Mpumalanga; used mainly to generate electricity and for export

How mining works

Mining is the process of extracting valuable minerals or other geological materials from the Earth. There are two broad extraction methods:

  • Surface (open-pit) mining: Used when the mineral deposit is close to the surface. Layers of soil and rock are removed to expose and extract the resource. Generally cheaper and safer, but disturbs a larger surface area.
  • Underground mining: Used when the mineral deposit lies deep below the surface. Shafts and tunnels are dug to reach and extract the resource. More expensive and higher risk for workers, but disturbs less surface area. Many of South Africa's deep gold mines are among the deepest in the world.

Economic importance and impacts of mining

Mining brings both significant benefits and significant costs, which need to be weighed carefully.

Economic benefits

  • Creates hundreds of thousands of jobs
  • Major contributor to South Africa's GDP and export earnings
  • Supports related industries (transport, equipment, processing)
  • Provides raw materials for construction, technology, and jewellery

Environmental & social impacts

  • Disturbs and can permanently alter land and ecosystems
  • Acid mine drainage: water reacting with exposed sulphide minerals becomes acidic and can pollute rivers and groundwater — a serious issue in parts of South Africa (e.g. the Witwatersrand)
  • Dust and air quality impacts near mining areas
  • Risk to worker health and safety, particularly in underground mining
Rehabilitation: Mining companies are increasingly required to rehabilitate mined land after operations end — refilling pits, treating polluted water, and replanting vegetation — to reduce long-term environmental damage while still allowing the economic benefits of mining to be realised.
