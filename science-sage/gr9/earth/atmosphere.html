Earth's Atmosphere

From the air we breathe to the auroras at the edge of space — explore the layered blanket that makes life on Earth possible.

Composition of the Atmosphere

The atmosphere is the layer of gases surrounding Earth, held in place by gravity. It is a mixture of gases, mainly:

  • Nitrogen (N₂): 78% — the most abundant gas; relatively unreactive, dilutes oxygen to safe levels
  • Oxygen (O₂): 21% — essential for respiration in animals and combustion
  • Argon (Ar): ~0.9% — an inert noble gas
  • Carbon dioxide (CO₂): ~0.04% — essential for photosynthesis; key greenhouse gas
  • Other gases: water vapour, neon, helium, methane, ozone (trace amounts)
Easy to remember: N₂ = 78%, O₂ = 21%, rest = 1% (Ar + CO₂ + others)

The Four Main Layers

The atmosphere is divided into layers based on how temperature changes with altitude. Temperature does NOT decrease steadily — it alternately decreases and increases depending on the layer.

LayerAltitudeTemperature trendKey features
Troposphere0–12 kmDecreases with altitude (from ~15°C to -56°C)All weather occurs here; contains ~75% of atmospheric mass; humans live here
Stratosphere12–50 kmIncreases with altitude (up to ~0°C)Contains the ozone layer (20–35 km); absorbs UV radiation; jet aircraft fly here
Mesosphere50–80 kmDecreases with altitude (down to -90°C)Coldest layer; most meteors burn up here; noctilucent clouds form here
Thermosphere80–700 kmIncreases sharply (up to 2 000°C+)Very thin air; auroras occur here; International Space Station orbits in lower thermosphere; radio waves reflect off this layer
Memory tip for the layers: The Strato-Mesosphere Thermo — or think of temperature: Down, Up, Down, Up as you rise through Troposphere → Stratosphere → Mesosphere → Thermosphere.

Air Pressure

Air pressure is the weight of the air column above a point. At sea level, air pressure is about 101 325 Pa (1 atmosphere). As altitude increases, there is less air above, so pressure decreases. At 12 km (top of troposphere), pressure is only about 20% of sea level pressure. At 50 km, it is less than 0.1%.

This is why aeroplanes must pressurise their cabins — at cruising altitude the air is too thin to breathe without assistance.

The Ozone Layer

The ozone layer is a region in the stratosphere (about 20–35 km up) where ozone (O₃) is concentrated. Ozone absorbs most of the Sun's harmful ultraviolet (UV) radiation, particularly UV-B and UV-C rays.

  • Without the ozone layer, UV radiation would damage DNA and cause skin cancer, cataracts, and harm to plants and marine life.
  • Chemicals called CFCs (chlorofluorocarbons) — once used in refrigerators and aerosol sprays — were destroying the ozone layer, causing an "ozone hole" over Antarctica.
  • The Montreal Protocol (1987) banned CFCs worldwide; the ozone layer is slowly recovering.

The Greenhouse Effect

The natural greenhouse effect keeps Earth warm enough to support life. Here is how it works:

  • Short-wave solar radiation passes through the atmosphere and warms Earth's surface.
  • Earth's surface radiates heat as long-wave infrared (heat) radiation.
  • Greenhouse gases (CO₂, water vapour, methane, nitrous oxide) absorb this outgoing heat and re-radiate some of it back toward Earth's surface.
  • This raises the average surface temperature to about +15°C (without any greenhouse effect it would be about -18°C).

The enhanced greenhouse effect is the problem: human activities (burning fossil fuels, deforestation) release extra CO₂ and other greenhouse gases, trapping more heat than normal and causing global temperatures to rise — global warming, which leads to climate change.

Key distinction: The natural greenhouse effect is essential for life. The enhanced greenhouse effect from extra human-produced gases is causing dangerous climate change. Do not confuse the two.
