Classification of Matter
Learn to classify all matter into pure substances and mixtures, distinguish elements from compounds, and select appropriate separation techniques.
National Senior Certificate

What is Matter?

Matter is anything that has mass and occupies space (volume). All physical substances — from air to iron to water — are matter. Matter can be classified in several ways based on its composition and properties.

Elements

An element is a pure substance that cannot be broken down into simpler substances by ordinary chemical means. Each element is made up of one type of atom. There are approximately 118 known elements, all found in the Periodic Table.

Examples: Hydrogen (H), Oxygen (O), Iron (Fe), Carbon (C), Gold (Au), Copper (Cu)

Compounds

A compound is a pure substance made of two or more elements chemically combined in fixed ratios. Compounds have properties that are completely different from the elements that form them, and can only be broken down by chemical means (not physical).

Example: Water (H₂O) is a compound of hydrogen and oxygen in a 2:1 ratio. It has completely different properties from the explosive gas H₂ or the reactive gas O₂ that make it up.

Classification of Mixtures

Mixtures are classified based on whether their composition is uniform throughout:

  • Homogeneous mixture (solution): Uniform composition throughout; cannot distinguish components by eye. Examples: salt water, alloys (steel, bronze), air, vinegar. The dissolved substance is the solute; what dissolves it is the solvent.
  • Heterogeneous mixture: Non-uniform; components are visibly distinct or in different phases. Examples: sand and water, oil and water, salad, granite.
Note: Colloids (e.g. milk, fog) and suspensions (e.g. muddy water) are intermediate — they appear homogeneous but particles are larger than in true solutions.

Physical vs Chemical Properties

  • Physical properties: Observable/measurable without changing chemical composition. Examples: colour, state of matter, density, melting point, boiling point, solubility, electrical conductivity.
  • Chemical properties: Describe how a substance changes its composition during a reaction. Examples: flammability, reactivity with acids, ability to rust, combustibility.

Properties can also be classified as:

  • Intensive: Do NOT depend on amount of substance (density, melting point, boiling point, colour).
  • Extensive: DO depend on amount (mass, volume, length, energy).

States of Matter & Density

Matter exists in four states: solid, liquid, gas, and plasma. The kinetic molecular theory explains states in terms of particle arrangement, energy, and movement.

ρ = m / V     (density = mass ÷ volume; unit: kg/m³ or g/cm³)
Water: ρ = 1.00 g/cm³ | Gold: ρ = 19.3 g/cm³ | Air: ρ ≈ 0.0013 g/cm³ | Iron: ρ = 7.87 g/cm³
IEB Extension — Colligative Properties & Alloys

Colligative properties depend only on the number of solute particles dissolved, not their identity:

  • Boiling point elevation: Adding a solute raises the boiling point of the solvent (e.g. antifreeze in a car radiator).
  • Freezing point depression: Adding a solute lowers the freezing point (e.g. salt on icy roads).

Alloys are solid solutions (homogeneous mixtures of metals). Common examples:

AlloyComponentsUse
SteelFe + C (0.2–2%)Construction, tools
BronzeCu + Sn (≈10%)Statues, bearings
BrassCu + Zn (10–45%)Musical instruments, plumbing
18-carat goldAu (75%) + Cu/Ag (25%)Jewellery

Raoult's Law (qualitative): The vapour pressure of a solution is lower than that of the pure solvent. This explains why dissolved solutes raise the boiling point — more energy is needed for solvent molecules to escape from solution.

Question 1 — Classification
Which of the following is a pure substance?
Correct! Distilled water is a pure compound with a fixed composition (H₂O) and a definite boiling point of 100°C. Air, sea water, and stainless steel are all mixtures.
Air is a mixture of gases. Sea water contains dissolved salts. Stainless steel is an alloy (mixture). Only distilled water (H₂O) is a pure compound with fixed composition.
Question 2 — Elements vs Compounds
Carbon dioxide (CO₂) is best described as:
Correct! CO₂ is a compound — it contains carbon and oxygen atoms chemically bonded together in a fixed 1:2 ratio. It has different properties from pure C or pure O₂.
CO₂ is a compound: two elements (C and O) chemically combined in a fixed ratio (1:2). The components cannot be separated physically, and CO₂ has properties different from its constituent elements.
Question 3 — Separation Technique
A learner needs to separate salt (sodium chloride) from a salt-water solution. Which technique is most appropriate?
Correct! Salt dissolves in water (it's a homogeneous mixture). Evaporation drives off the water as vapour, leaving solid salt behind. Filtration only works for insoluble solids.
Filtration won't work because the salt is dissolved (no solid particles to filter). Evaporation is correct — heat the solution until all the water evaporates, leaving dry salt crystals.
Question 4 — Properties
Which of the following is an intensive property?
Correct! Density (ρ = m/V) is intensive — it does not change with the amount of substance. Whether you have 1 g or 1 kg of gold, the density is always 19.3 g/cm³. Mass, volume, and length all change with amount (extensive).
Density is intensive — it's the ratio of mass to volume, so it stays the same regardless of the amount of substance. Mass, volume, and length are extensive (they depend on how much you have).
Question 5 — Homogeneous vs Heterogeneous
Which of the following is a heterogeneous mixture?
Correct! Granite is heterogeneous — you can see the different mineral crystals (quartz, feldspar, mica) with the naked eye. Brass, wine, and air are homogeneous (uniform composition throughout).
Granite is heterogeneous — distinct mineral grains are visible to the naked eye. Brass (alloy), wine (solution), and air (gas mixture) are all homogeneous — uniform throughout.
Question 6 — Density Calculation
A rectangular block of metal has a mass of 540 g and dimensions 3 cm × 4 cm × 5 cm. What is its density?
Correct! V = 3 × 4 × 5 = 60 cm³. ρ = m/V = 540/60 = 9 g/cm³. This is close to the density of copper (8.96 g/cm³)!
First find the volume: V = 3 × 4 × 5 = 60 cm³. Then ρ = m/V = 540 g ÷ 60 cm³ = 9 g/cm³.

IEB Question 1 — Colligative Properties
Why does adding salt to water raise its boiling point, and what term describes this phenomenon?
Correct! Dissolved particles (ions from NaCl) reduce the vapour pressure of the solution (Raoult's Law). Since a lower vapour pressure means more energy is needed to reach atmospheric pressure, the boiling point rises. This is a colligative property — boiling point elevation.
Dissolved solute particles reduce vapour pressure (Raoult's Law). Because vapour pressure is lower, more energy is needed to boil the solution → boiling point elevation. This is a colligative property depending only on the number of particles, not what they are.
IEB Question 2 — Alloy Classification
Bronze is an alloy of copper (Cu) and tin (Sn). How should bronze be classified in terms of the matter classification hierarchy, and why?
Correct! Alloys are solid solutions — homogeneous mixtures where the composition can vary (e.g. bronze can be 85–95% Cu). The metals are not chemically bonded, so bronze is a mixture, not a compound. Its uniform appearance makes it homogeneous.
Bronze is a homogeneous mixture (solid solution). The Cu:Sn ratio is variable, so it's not a compound (which requires fixed ratios). At the atomic level, the metals are uniformly distributed, so it's homogeneous, not heterogeneous.
