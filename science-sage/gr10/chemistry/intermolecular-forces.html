Intramolecular vs Intermolecular Forces

It is essential to distinguish between two very different types of forces in chemistry:

  • Intramolecular forces: forces WITHIN a molecule — these are the covalent bonds, ionic bonds, or metallic bonds that hold atoms together within a substance. They determine the chemical properties of the substance (what reactions it undergoes, what products form).
  • Intermolecular forces (IMF): forces BETWEEN separate molecules — these hold molecules together in a bulk sample of liquid or solid. They do NOT create new bonds; they are attractions between neighbouring molecules. They determine the physical properties of the substance (melting point, boiling point, viscosity, surface tension, solubility).
Key distinction: When water boils, the intermolecular forces (hydrogen bonds between H₂O molecules) are overcome — NOT the O–H covalent bonds within the water molecules. The O–H bonds remain intact in steam.

Type 1 — London Dispersion Forces (Van der Waals Forces)

London dispersion forces (LDF) are present in ALL molecules — both polar and non-polar. They arise because the electron cloud around any atom is constantly moving. At any instant, the electrons may be distributed unevenly, creating a TEMPORARY dipole. This temporary dipole induces a dipole in neighbouring molecules, and a weak attraction results.

  • Present in ALL substances (the only IMF in non-polar molecules)
  • Weakest type of intermolecular force
  • Strength increases with: (1) molar mass — more electrons means larger, more polarisable electron clouds; (2) surface area — more contact between molecules means stronger LDF
  • Examples: noble gases (liquid He, liquid Ar), non-polar molecules (I₂, Cl₂, Br₂, CH₄, all alkanes)
LDF: strength ∝ molar mass and surface area. More electrons → bigger instantaneous dipoles → stronger LDF

Type 2 — Dipole-Dipole Interactions

Dipole-dipole interactions occur between polar molecules. A polar molecule has a PERMANENT dipole — one end is slightly positive (δ+) and the other is slightly negative (δ−), because of unequal sharing of electrons in a polar bond combined with an asymmetric shape.

When two polar molecules are near each other, they align so that the δ+ end of one molecule is attracted to the δ− end of another. This electrostatic attraction is a dipole-dipole interaction.

  • Stronger than LDF for molecules of similar molar mass
  • Only between polar molecules
  • Examples: HCl, SO₂, CHCl₃ (chloroform), acetone (CH₃COCH₃)
Note: LDF also acts between polar molecules — dipole-dipole interactions are IN ADDITION to LDF, not instead of them.

Type 3 — Hydrogen Bonding (The Strongest IMF)

Hydrogen bonding is a special, unusually strong type of dipole-dipole interaction. It occurs when a hydrogen atom is covalently bonded to a highly electronegative, small atom (N, O, or F). The enormous electronegativity difference creates a very strong δ+ on the H atom. This H atom is then attracted to a lone pair on a N, O, or F atom of an ADJACENT molecule.

X−H ··· Y    (where X and Y are N, O, or F; the dashed line ··· is the H-bond)
  • Only occurs when H is bonded directly to N, O, or F
  • Much stronger than ordinary dipole-dipole forces
  • Still weaker than covalent bonds (about 1/10 the strength)
  • Explains anomalously high boiling points of H₂O, HF, and NH₃

Anomalous Properties of Water

Water (H₂O) is the most important example of hydrogen bonding:

  • Unusually high boiling point (100°C) for its small molar mass (18 g·mol⁻¹) — due to strong H-bonding
  • High surface tension — H-bonds hold surface molecules together tightly
  • High specific heat capacity — requires lots of energy to break H-bonds
  • Ice is LESS DENSE than liquid water — in ice, H₂O molecules form a rigid hexagonal lattice with H-bonds holding them further apart than in liquid water. This is why ice floats.
  • Biological importance: DNA base pairs held together by H-bonds (A–T: 2 H-bonds; G–C: 3 H-bonds); protein secondary structure (alpha-helices and beta-sheets); cellulose structure in plant cell walls

IEB Extension — Ion-Dipole Forces & Applications

Ion-dipole forces occur between an ion (from an ionic compound) and a polar molecule (solvent). When NaCl dissolves in water, the Na⁺ ions attract the δ− oxygen ends of water molecules, and Cl⁻ ions attract the δ+ hydrogen ends. Each ion is surrounded by a "hydration shell" of water molecules. The ion-dipole attraction must overcome the ionic lattice energy — this is possible because many ion-dipole interactions act together, releasing sufficient energy (hydration enthalpy).

Soap and detergent molecules (e.g. sodium stearate) have a dual structure:

  • Hydrophilic head (ionic carboxylate end, –COO⁻Na⁺): attracted to water molecules via ion-dipole forces
  • Hydrophobic tail (long non-polar hydrocarbon chain): dissolves non-polar grease/oil via LDF

When washing with soap, the hydrophobic tails dissolve into grease particles while the hydrophilic heads remain in the water. This creates micelles — spherical clusters with grease inside and ionic heads on the outside — that are easily rinsed away in water.

Biological membranes (phospholipid bilayers) use the same principle: phospholipid molecules have a polar head and two non-polar fatty acid tails. In water, they spontaneously form a bilayer with tails facing inward (away from water) and heads facing outward (towards water), forming the basis of all cell membranes.

Question 1 (2 marks)
Which type of intermolecular force is present in ALL molecules, including non-polar ones?
London dispersion forces (LDF) arise from temporary dipoles due to electron movement and are present in ALL substances — polar and non-polar. They are the only IMF in non-polar molecules like I₂ or CH₄.
Question 2 (2 marks)
The boiling point of H₂O is 100°C while H₂S (a larger molecule) boils at −60°C. The BEST explanation is:
H₂O forms strong hydrogen bonds (O–H···O) because oxygen is highly electronegative and small. Sulfur is less electronegative and larger, so H₂S only has dipole-dipole and LDF — much weaker. This gives H₂O a much higher BP despite its smaller size.
Question 3 (2 marks)
Which of the following molecules can form hydrogen bonds with water?
Hydrogen bonding requires H bonded to N, O, or F. NH₃ has H atoms bonded to nitrogen (highly electronegative) and lone pairs on N, so NH₃ can both donate and accept H-bonds with water. CH₄, CO₂, and CCl₄ cannot form H-bonds.
Question 4 (1 mark)
Ice is less dense than liquid water because:
In ice, hydrogen bonds lock water molecules into a hexagonal lattice structure where molecules are held further apart than in liquid water. This makes ice less dense, which is why ice floats on water — a property crucial for aquatic life surviving in frozen lakes.
Question 5 (1 mark)
Iodine (I₂) is soluble in hexane (C₆H₁₄) but insoluble in water. This is best explained by:
'Like dissolves like': non-polar I₂ dissolves in non-polar hexane because both have LDF as their only IMF. Water is polar and forms hydrogen bonds — I₂ cannot interact with water molecules strongly enough to dissolve.
Question 6 (2 marks)
Going from HCl to HBr to HI, the boiling point increases. The main reason is:
HCl, HBr, and HI all have dipole-dipole forces and LDF, but NO hydrogen bonding (H is not bonded to N, O or F here). As molar mass increases (HCl 36.5 → HI 128 g·mol⁻¹), the larger electron cloud creates stronger LDF, raising the boiling point.
IEB Question 1 IEB (2 marks)
When sodium chloride (NaCl) dissolves in water, the process is called hydration. What type of intermolecular force is responsible for holding the water molecules around the Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions?
Ion-dipole forces act between the charged ions (Na⁺ or Cl⁻) and the polar water molecules (which have δ+ on H and δ− on O). Na⁺ attracts the δ− oxygen of water; Cl⁻ attracts the δ+ hydrogen. Many of these ion-dipole interactions collectively provide enough energy to overcome the lattice energy of NaCl, allowing it to dissolve.
IEB Question 2 IEB (1 mark)
A soap molecule has a long non-polar hydrocarbon tail and an ionic carboxylate head (−COO⁻Na⁺). How does this dual structure allow soap to remove grease from surfaces when washed with water?
Soap molecules form micelles: the hydrophobic (non-polar) hydrocarbon tails dissolve into the grease particle through London dispersion forces, while the hydrophilic (ionic) carboxylate heads remain in the surrounding water through ion-dipole attractions. The grease is trapped inside the micelle and the whole structure is water-soluble — easily rinsed away.
