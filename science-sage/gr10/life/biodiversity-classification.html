Biodiversity & Classification

Earth holds an enormous variety of life. See how scientists organise that diversity into a nested hierarchy, and explore the five kingdoms of living things.

Enormous biodiversity, one system for organising it

Earth holds a huge variety of life forms and ways of living. To make sense of this diversity, scientists group organisms based on shared features into a classification scheme — a nested hierarchy, much like sorting everyday objects into groups and subgroups based on shared similarities.

Southern Africa is especially rich in biodiversity, with many indigenous (naturally occurring) and endemic (found nowhere else) species.

Naming species: the binomial system

Scientists use a binomial (two-part) naming system, developed by the Swedish scientist Carl Linnaeus, in which every species gets a unique two-word Latin name (genus + species). Latin is used because it's a "dead" language that doesn't change meaning over time, giving every scientist in the world — regardless of their home language — the same, stable name for a species.

The five-kingdom system

One widely-used classification system divides all living organisms into five kingdoms. A key distinction across these kingdoms is between prokaryotes (cells with no nucleus, e.g. Monera/Bacteria) and eukaryotes (cells with a true nucleus — all other kingdoms).

Explore all five kingdoms and their defining features in the Explore tab.

As scientific knowledge grows, classification systems continue to change and improve — the five-kingdom system is one of several currently accepted approaches, not a final, unchangeable truth.
