Chemistry of Life

All living things are built from atoms and molecules. Explore the inorganic and organic compounds that make up cells — water, carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, nucleic acids — and how enzymes control the chemistry of life.

From atoms to organisms

All living organisms are made of atoms that combine to form molecules. These molecules make up the basic units of life — cells — which are organised into tissues, organs, and eventually whole organisms. To understand how cells work, we first need to understand the chemistry they're built from.

Biological compounds are grouped into two broad categories:

  • Inorganic compounds — do not contain both carbon and hydrogen bonded together as their main structural feature. In biology, the key inorganic compound is water.
  • Organic compounds — built around a "backbone" of carbon (C), hydrogen (H), and oxygen (O) atoms (some also contain nitrogen, phosphorus, or sulfur). These are the molecules of life: carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids.

Water — the inorganic compound of life

A water molecule (H₂O) consists of 2 hydrogen atoms bonded to 1 oxygen atom. Water makes up the majority of every living cell and is essential for life:

  • It's the solvent in which most chemical reactions in the cell take place.
  • It transports dissolved substances around the body (e.g. in blood plasma or plant xylem/phloem sap).
  • It helps regulate temperature — water absorbs and releases heat slowly, keeping conditions inside cells stable.
  • It's a reactant in many biological reactions, such as photosynthesis.
Minerals are the other main inorganic compounds in living organisms — elements like calcium, iron, and potassium play structural roles (e.g. calcium in bone) and are needed in small amounts for healthy cell function.

Carbohydrates — energy and structure

Carbohydrates are built from carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen, and come in three sizes:

TypeMeaningExamples
MonosaccharidesSingle sugar unitGlucose, fructose
DisaccharidesTwo sugar units joinedSucrose, maltose
PolysaccharidesMany sugar units joined in a chainStarch, cellulose, glycogen

Carbohydrates are mainly used as an energy source (glucose is broken down during respiration) and, in the case of cellulose, provide structural support in plant cell walls.

Lipids — fats and oils

A lipid molecule is made of 1 glycerol molecule bonded to 3 fatty acid chains. Fatty acids can be:

  • Saturated — no double bonds between carbon atoms; usually solid at room temperature (e.g. animal fats). Linked to raised cholesterol and heart disease risk when eaten in excess.
  • Unsaturated — contain double bonds; usually liquid at room temperature (e.g. plant oils).

Lipids store energy (more energy-dense than carbohydrates), insulate the body, protect organs, and form the cell membrane.

Proteins — the workhorses of the cell

Proteins are built from smaller units called amino acids, containing carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, and nitrogen (some also contain sulfur or phosphorus). Proteins have many roles: structural (e.g. keratin in hair), transport (e.g. haemoglobin), and — most importantly for this lesson — as enzymes.

Proteins are sensitive to temperature and pH. Extreme conditions change their shape — this is called denaturation, and it stops the protein from working.

Nucleic acids — the genetic code

DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) and RNA (ribonucleic acid) are nucleic acids built from carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, and phosphorus. They store and transmit the genetic instructions that direct how a cell is built and how it functions. You'll study their detailed structure in Grade 12 — for now, it's enough to know they exist and what elements they contain.

Cells also require small amounts of vitamins (such as A, the B vitamins, C, D, and E) to function normally.

Enzymes — biological catalysts

Enzymes are proteins that speed up chemical reactions in the cell without being used up themselves. Each enzyme has a uniquely shaped active site that fits a specific substrate molecule — this is called the lock-and-key model.

  • The substrate binds to the enzyme's active site.
  • The enzyme speeds up the reaction, converting the substrate into product(s).
  • The product is released, and the enzyme is free to bind another substrate molecule.
Enzymes work fastest at an optimum temperature and pH. Below the optimum, reactions are slow because molecules move less. Above the optimum, the enzyme's shape starts to change (denature) — the active site no longer fits the substrate, and the enzyme stops working. Try this yourself in the Explore tab!
