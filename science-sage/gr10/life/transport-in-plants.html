Support & Transport in Plants

How does water get from the roots to the top of a tree? Explore transpiration — the process that pulls water up through the xylem — and see how temperature, humidity, light and wind affect its rate.

Two transport systems, one plant

Plants have two separate transport tissues, both found in the roots, stems, and leaves:

TissueTransportsDirection
XylemWater and dissolved mineralsUpward — roots to leaves
PhloemManufactured food (sugars) — translocationFrom leaves to all other parts

Transpiration — the engine that pulls water up

Transpiration is the loss of water vapour from a plant's surface, mostly through tiny pores (stomata) in the leaves. As water evaporates from the leaf, it creates a "pull" that draws more water up through the xylem, all the way from the roots — a bit like sucking on a straw.

The pathway is: water uptake by root hairs → movement up the xylem → evaporation from the leaves (transpiration). This constant flow also carries dissolved minerals to where they're needed.

What affects the rate of transpiration?

  • Temperature — higher temperature increases the rate of evaporation, speeding up transpiration.
  • Light intensity — more light means stomata open wider (for photosynthesis), increasing water loss.
  • Humidity — high humidity in the air reduces the concentration gradient for evaporation, slowing transpiration.
  • Wind — moving air sweeps away water vapour near the leaf surface, increasing the rate of transpiration.
Scientists measure transpiration rate in the lab using a potometer, which tracks how quickly a leafy shoot draws up water.

Growth rings — a record in the wood

As a tree undergoes secondary growth each year, it produces a new ring of wood. Counting these annual rings in a cut tree trunk can reveal both the tree's age and clues about past climate conditions — wider rings generally indicate a favourable growing season.
