Biosphere to Ecosystems

From the whole biosphere down to a single ecosystem: explore abiotic and biotic factors, trophic levels, and how energy flows — and is lost — as it passes up a food chain.

From biosphere to ecosystem

The biosphere is the global "layer of life" — the sum of every ecosystem on Earth, made up of the atmosphere, hydrosphere (water), and lithosphere (land) all interconnected. Within the biosphere, an ecosystem is a smaller unit: a community of living organisms interacting with each other and with their non-living environment in one area.

Abiotic and biotic factors

Every ecosystem is shaped by two kinds of factors:

Factor typeExamples
Abiotic (non-living)Temperature, light, water, soil (pH, texture, humus content), physiographic factors (slope, altitude, aspect)
Biotic (living)All the organisms in the ecosystem, and their interactions with each other

Trophic levels — who eats whom

Organisms in an ecosystem occupy different trophic (feeding) levels:

  • Producers — plants and other organisms that make their own food (usually via photosynthesis).
  • Consumers — organisms that eat other organisms: herbivores (eat plants), carnivores (eat other animals), and omnivores (eat both).
  • Decomposers — break down dead organisms and waste, returning nutrients to the ecosystem.

Energy flow through an ecosystem

Energy enters an ecosystem from the sun and flows through it via food chains: producer → primary consumer → secondary consumer → tertiary consumer. At each step, a large amount of energy is lost (mostly as heat), so less energy is available at each successive trophic level. This is why food chains rarely have more than 4–5 levels, and why ecosystems can support far more producers than top predators — visualised as a food/energy pyramid.
