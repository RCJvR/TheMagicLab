Plant & Animal Tissues

Cells don't work alone — they group into tissues, each specialised for a particular job. Explore the seven plant tissue types and see how tissue specialisation gets underway in animals too.

What is a tissue?

A tissue is a group of similar cells, working together, that perform a particular function. Individual cells become specialised (differentiated) for a specific job — this is called cell differentiation.

The seven plant tissues

Plants have several distinct tissue types, each built for a specific role. Explore each one in detail in the Explore tab.

TissueFunction
MeristematicUndifferentiated, actively dividing cells found at root and shoot tips — responsible for growth
EpidermisA protective outer layer covering the whole plant
ParenchymaPacking and storage tissue; also carries out photosynthesis in leaves
CollenchymaFlexible support in young, growing stems
SclerenchymaRigid support; cells are dead at maturity, with thick, hardened walls
XylemTransports water and minerals; made of dead, hollow cells forming tubes
PhloemTransports manufactured food (sugars); made of living cells
In a stem or root, these tissues are arranged in specific patterns that can be seen in cross-section under a microscope — you'll study this arrangement in more detail in the next lesson on transport in plants.

Animal tissues — an introduction

Just like plants, animal cells differentiate into specialised tissues suited to a particular structure and function. This is only a brief introduction here — you'll explore specific animal tissue types (such as bone, cartilage, and muscle) in more depth in the next lesson on support systems in animals.
