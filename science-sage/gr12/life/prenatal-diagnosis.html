Prenatal diagnosis and trisomies

Doctors can examine an unborn baby’s chromosomes. Learn the main tests, how to read a karyotype, and how trisomies arise.

Why test before birth?

Some genetic disorders, especially trisomies (three copies of one chromosome instead of two), can be detected before birth. Results help parents and doctors plan care, prepare for the birth, and make informed decisions.

Prenatal diagnosis checks for chromosome number and structure. Many single-gene disorders (such as cystic fibrosis) can also be tested for, if a family has a known risk.

Prenatal tests

  • Amniocentesis — a fine needle is inserted through the abdominal wall into the amniotic sac to draw out a small sample of amniotic fluid. The fluid contains fetal cells, which are grown in the laboratory and their chromosomes examined. It is usually done from about week 15 of pregnancy.
  • Chorionic villus sampling (CVS) — a small sample of the chorionic villi (placental tissue) is taken, usually at about week 10 to 13. Results come sooner than from amniocentesis.
Risk: both tests carry a small risk of miscarriage. They are offered when the benefit of knowing the result is judged to outweigh that risk, and the parents give informed consent.

Reading a karyotype

A karyotype is an ordered picture of a person’s chromosomes, arranged in homologous pairs by size, centromere position and banding pattern. A normal human karyotype has 46 chromosomes (23 pairs): 22 autosomal pairs and one pair of sex chromosomes (XX in a female, XY in a male).

✏️ Worked example
1Count the chromosomes. The karyotype shows 47 chromosomes, with three copies of chromosome 21.
2Normal count = 46. An extra chromosome gives 47, so this is a numerical abnormality: trisomy.
3Because the extra chromosome is number 21, the diagnosis is trisomy 21 (Down syndrome).

Trisomies and non-disjunction

TrisomyExtra chromosomeCondition
Trisomy 21Chromosome 21Down syndrome
Trisomy 18Chromosome 18Edwards syndrome
Trisomy 13Chromosome 13Patau syndrome

Trisomies usually arise from non-disjunction: a pair of homologous chromosomes (or sister chromatids) fails to separate during meiosis. One gamete then has an extra copy, and after fertilisation the zygote has three copies. The risk of non-disjunction in the mother increases with her age, which is why age is a key risk factor.
