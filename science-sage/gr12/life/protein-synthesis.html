Protein synthesis and mutations

Follow a gene from DNA to protein: transcription in the nucleus, translation at the ribosome with mRNA and tRNA, and what happens to the protein when the DNA changes.

Transcription

Transcription happens in the nucleus:

  1. The DNA double helix unwinds and the hydrogen bonds between bases break.
  2. One strand acts as the template.
  3. Free RNA nucleotides pair with the template bases (A–U, T–A, C–G, G–C) to form a strand of mRNA.
  4. The mRNA leaves the nucleus through a nuclear pore and moves to a ribosome.

Translation

Translation happens at a ribosome in the cytoplasm:

  1. The mRNA attaches to the ribosome. Every three bases on the mRNA form a codon.
  2. Each tRNA molecule carries a specific amino acid and has an anticodon that is complementary to a codon.
  3. tRNA anticodons pair with the mRNA codons in order, bringing amino acids in the correct sequence.
  4. Peptide bonds form between adjacent amino acids, building a polypeptide chain until a stop codon is reached.

Mutations

A mutation is a change in the sequence of bases in DNA.

  • Substitution — one base is replaced by another. It may change one amino acid, or none if the new codon codes for the same amino acid.
  • Insertion or deletion — a base is added or removed. This shifts the reading frame (a frameshift), changing every codon after it, so the protein is usually non-functional.
  • Chromosome mutations — changes in chromosome structure or number, such as trisomy from non-disjunction.
✏️ Effect of a substitution
1In the example above, the third DNA triplet CCG changes to CTG.
2The mRNA codon changes from GGC to GAC, so glycine is replaced by aspartic acid.
3A different amino acid can change the shape of the protein and therefore its function.

Mutations can be harmful (for example haemophilia), harmless (no change in the protein), or useful (a new variation that increases survival, which natural selection can act on).
