Evolution — Evidence and Human Evolution

Four independent lines of evidence all point to the same conclusion: species share common ancestors. Explore the hominin timeline leading to modern humans.

Evidence for evolution

Evolutionary theory is supported by multiple, independent lines of evidence that all converge on the same conclusion — that living species share common ancestry and have changed over time.

  • The fossil record — fossils show a chronological sequence of change, including transitional forms that share features of two different groups (e.g. Archaeopteryx, showing both reptilian and bird-like features), demonstrating gradual change over millions of years.
  • Comparative anatomy — homologous structures (same basic structure, inherited from a common ancestor, but adapted for different functions — e.g. the forelimb bones of a human, whale, and bat) provide strong evidence of shared ancestry. Analogous structures (similar function but different origin, e.g. the wings of a bird and an insect) arise from convergent evolution, not common ancestry.
  • Molecular/DNA evidence — species that are more closely related share a greater percentage of similar DNA and protein sequences. The degree of genetic similarity between species reflects how recently they shared a common ancestor.
  • Biogeography — the geographical distribution of species (e.g. unique marsupials in Australia, isolated for millions of years) reflects patterns of migration, isolation, and independent evolution.
Homologous vs analogous: homologous structures = same origin, different function (evidence of common ancestry). Analogous structures = different origin, similar function (evidence of convergent evolution, not common ancestry).

Human evolution — the hominin lineage

Fossil evidence shows a lineage of hominins (the group including modern humans and our extinct close relatives) evolving in Africa over millions of years, showing clear trends over time.

TrendChange over time
Brain sizeSteady increase from early hominins to modern humans
PostureIncreasing bipedalism (walking upright on two legs)
Tool useFrom simple stone tools to increasingly complex technology
Skull/jawReduction in jaw size and brow ridge prominence

Key stages in the hominin lineage include (in order): Australopithecus (small-brained, bipedal) → Homo habilis ("handy man", first stone tool use) → Homo erectus (larger brain, controlled use of fire, first to migrate out of Africa) → Homo sapiens (modern humans, complex language, art, and culture).

The "Out of Africa" hypothesis

Genetic and fossil evidence strongly supports the "Out of Africa" hypothesis — that Homo sapiens originated in Africa roughly 200,000 years ago, then migrated outward in waves to populate the rest of the world, gradually replacing other hominin species (such as Neanderthals) that had migrated out of Africa earlier.

Note: human evolution is not a single straight "ladder" — several hominin species often existed at the same time, and the lineage is better pictured as a branching bush than a straight line.
