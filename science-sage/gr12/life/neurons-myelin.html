Neurons, impulses and synapses

Label a neuron, explain how the myelin sheath speeds up impulses, and describe how a synapse passes the message on in one direction only.

Structure of a neuron

  • Cell body — contains the nucleus and most organelles.
  • Dendrites — short branches that carry impulses towards the cell body.
  • Axon — a long fibre that carries impulses away from the cell body.
  • Myelin sheath — a fatty, insulating layer around the axon.
  • Nodes of Ranvier — gaps in the myelin sheath.
  • Axon terminals (synaptic knobs) — release neurotransmitters into the synapse.
Type of neuronFunction
Sensory (afferent)Carries impulses from receptors to the central nervous system
Interneuron (connector)Links sensory and motor neurons inside the central nervous system
Motor (efferent)Carries impulses from the central nervous system to effectors (muscles, glands)

The nerve impulse and myelin

A nerve impulse is an electrochemical message: a change in charge across the membrane that travels along the axon. Myelin is made by Schwann cells in the peripheral nervous system and by oligodendrocytes in the brain and spinal cord.

Myelin insulates the axon, so the impulse cannot pass along the membrane where myelin is present. Instead it jumps from one node of Ranvier to the next — saltatory conduction. This makes impulses in myelinated neurons much faster than in unmyelinated ones of the same diameter.

Multiple sclerosis: the immune system damages myelin in the central nervous system, so impulses slow down or are blocked, causing weakness, numbness and poor coordination.

The synapse

A synapse is the small gap between the axon terminal of one neuron and the next cell. When an impulse arrives:

  1. vesicles in the axon terminal release a neurotransmitter (for example acetylcholine) into the gap;
  2. the neurotransmitter diffuses across the gap and binds to receptors on the next neuron;
  3. this starts a new impulse in the next neuron;
  4. the neurotransmitter is broken down by enzymes, so the signal stops.

Synapses make sure impulses travel in one direction only, because only the axon terminal releases neurotransmitter and only the next cell has receptors. Many drugs act at synapses, by copying, blocking or preventing the breakdown of neurotransmitters.

Worked example: tracing a reflex

✏️ Touching a hot plate
1Receptors in the skin detect heat and start an impulse in a sensory neuron.
2The sensory neuron carries it to the spinal cord, where it crosses a synapse to an interneuron.
3The interneuron passes it across a synapse to a motor neuron.
4The motor neuron carries it to the arm muscle (effector), which contracts and pulls the hand away — before the brain is aware of the pain.
