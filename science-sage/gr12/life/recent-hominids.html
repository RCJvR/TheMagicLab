Hominid fossils and South African discoveries

Read a fossil skull for evidence of bipedalism and diet, compare brain volumes across hominids, and learn what Rising Star, Blombos and Wonderwerk caves tell us about our ancestors.

Reading a fossil skull

Palaeontologists compare skulls of apes, early hominids and modern humans. Key features and the trend towards modern humans:

FeatureAfrican apesModern humansWhat it shows
Foramen magnumTowards the backCentral, under the skullHead balanced on an upright spine: bipedalism
Brow ridgesProminentReducedChange in face and skull shape
Jaw (prognathism)ProtrudingFlat faceSmaller jaw and teeth, softer or cooked diet
CaninesLarge, with gaps (diastema)Small, no gapChange in diet and behaviour
CraniumSmall, lowLarge, roundedLarger brain

Brain volume across hominids

Genus / speciesApproximate brain volume
Australopithecus (e.g. A. africanus)400–500 cm³
Homo habilis550–680 cm³
Homo erectus750–1 250 cm³
Homo sapiensabout 1 350 cm³

The general trend is an increase in brain volume, linked to tool use, more complex behaviour and diet. The trend is not a straight line: some later species, such as Homo naledi, had small brains.

South African sites

  • Taung (1924) — the Taung Child, the first Australopithecus africanus described, by Raymond Dart.
  • Sterkfontein — “Mrs Ples” (A. africanus, 1947) and “Little Foot”, a nearly complete australopithecine skeleton.
  • Malapa (2008) — Australopithecus sediba, with a mix of ape-like and human-like features.
  • Rising Star cave (found 2013, named 2015) — Homo naledi: a small brain (estimates vary, roughly 450–600 cm³) but human-like hands and feet. It lived about 236 000–335 000 years ago, overlapping in time with early Homo sapiens.

These sites are part of the Cradle of Humankind World Heritage Site and support the Out of Africa hypothesis, along with mitochondrial DNA evidence.

Evidence of behaviour: Blombos and Wonderwerk

  • Blombos Cave (Southern Cape coast) — engraved ochre pieces and perforated shell beads about 75 000 years old, and an ochre-processing kit about 100 000 years old. They show early symbolic behaviour in Homo sapiens, such as decoration and possibly communication through symbols.
  • Wonderwerk Cave (Northern Cape) — burnt bone and plant ash about one million years old, among the earliest evidence of controlled fire, associated with early Homo.
Why caves preserve evidence: they are sheltered from wind and rain, sediments build up in layers that can be dated, and the stable conditions slow decay. Excavated caves are often closed to the public to protect deposits that are still being studied.

Question 1 · (3 marks)
Describe how the position of the foramen magnum differs between apes and modern humans, and explain what this indicates.
Show memo
In apes the foramen magnum is towards the back of the skull; in humans it is central, under the skull ✓✓. This shows that the head was balanced on top of an upright spine, so the organism walked on two legs (bipedalism) ✓.

Question 3 · (3 marks)
Homo naledi had a small brain but lived at the same time as early Homo sapiens. Explain why this discovery is important.
Show memo
It shows that brain size did not increase in a single straight line ✓; species with small brains survived alongside larger-brained species ✓; so more than one hominid species lived in Africa at the same time ✓.
Question 4 · (3 marks)
Explain why ochre and shell beads from Blombos Cave are considered evidence of modern human behaviour.
Show memo
They were made or decorated deliberately, not for survival ✓; this shows symbolic thinking or decoration ✓, which is regarded as a feature of modern human culture ✓.
