Physical properties of organic compounds

Explain trends in boiling point, melting point and vapour pressure using intermolecular forces, and answer the investigation questions (variables, fair test, conclusions) that come with every data table.

Intermolecular forces in organic compounds

SeriesStrongest intermolecular force
Alkanes, alkenes, alkynesLondon (dispersion) forces only
Haloalkanes, aldehydes, ketones, estersDipole–dipole forces (plus London forces)
AlcoholsHydrogen bonding (one site per molecule)
Carboxylic acidsHydrogen bonding at two sites — molecules form dimers
📖 Definitions

Boiling point: the temperature at which the vapour pressure of a liquid equals the atmospheric (external) pressure.

Vapour pressure: the pressure exerted by a vapour at equilibrium with its liquid in a closed system.

Melting point: the temperature at which the solid and liquid phases of a substance are in equilibrium.

Stronger intermolecular forces → more energy needed to overcome them → higher boiling and melting points and lower vapour pressure.

The three trends

  1. Chain length (same series): longer chain → larger surface area / molecular size → stronger London forces → higher boiling point. Methanol 65 °C, ethanol 78 °C, propan-1-ol 97 °C, butan-1-ol 117 °C.
  2. Branching (chain isomers): more branches → more compact, smaller contact surface area → weaker London forces → lower boiling point. Pentane 36 °C, 2-methylbutane 28 °C, 2,2-dimethylpropane 10 °C.
  3. Functional group (similar molar mass): alkane < aldehyde/ketone < alcohol < carboxylic acid. Butane −1 °C, propanal 49 °C, propan-1-ol 97 °C, ethanoic acid 118 °C (all about 58–60 g·mol⁻¹).
✏️ Model answer: explaining a difference in boiling point
1Compare the structure: “Propan-1-ol has a hydroxyl group; propanal has a carbonyl group.”
2Compare the intermolecular forces: “Propan-1-ol has hydrogen bonds between molecules; propanal has dipole–dipole forces, which are weaker.”
3Compare the energy: “More energy is needed to overcome the intermolecular forces in propan-1-ol, so its boiling point is higher.”

Note: carboxylic acids boil higher than alcohols of similar molar mass because they form hydrogen bonds at two sites (dimers).

Investigation skills

Most questions on this topic present a table of data from an investigation. Learn this language:

  • Independent variable: what the investigator changes (chain length, type of functional group, number of branches).
  • Dependent variable: what is measured (boiling point, vapour pressure, time to evaporate).
  • Controlled variables: kept the same for a fair test — for example molar mass when comparing functional groups, or the functional group when comparing chain length.
  • Fair test: only the independent variable changes; explain why compounds of similar molar mass are chosen (so the strength of London forces is about the same).
  • Investigative question: “What is the relationship between chain length and boiling point of alcohols?”
  • Conclusion: link the variables in one sentence: “As the chain length increases, the boiling point increases.”
Vapour pressure and boiling point are opposite: the compound with the highest boiling point has the lowest vapour pressure at a given temperature. A volatile compound has weak intermolecular forces and a high vapour pressure.
