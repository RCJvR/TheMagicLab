Seasons & Day/Night

Discover why Earth has seasons, why days are longer in summer, and how our planet's tilt and orbit shape life as we know it.

Earth's Rotation and Revolution

Earth has two important movements:

  • Rotation: Earth spins on its own axis once every 24 hours. This gives us day and night — the side facing the Sun experiences day, the side facing away experiences night.
  • Revolution: Earth orbits (revolves) around the Sun once every 365.25 days (one year). The extra quarter day is why we add a leap day (29 February) every four years.
Common misconception: Seasons are NOT caused by Earth being closer to or farther from the Sun. In fact, Earth is slightly closer to the Sun in January (Southern Hemisphere summer). Seasons are caused by Earth's axial tilt.

Earth's Axial Tilt

Earth's axis is tilted at 23.5° relative to its orbital plane around the Sun. This tilt always points in the same direction in space (toward Polaris, the North Star). As Earth orbits the Sun, different hemispheres are tilted toward or away from the Sun at different times of year.

  • When a hemisphere is tilted toward the Sun: the Sun is higher in the sky, days are longer, sunlight is more concentrated → summer
  • When a hemisphere is tilted away from the Sun: the Sun is lower in the sky, days are shorter, sunlight is more spread out → winter
The Southern and Northern Hemispheres always have opposite seasons. When it is summer in South Africa (December), it is winter in Europe.

Solstice comes from the Latin "sol" (sun) + "sistere" (to stand still) — the Sun appears to reach its highest or lowest point and then reverse. Equinox means "equal night" — day and night are each approximately 12 hours everywhere on Earth.

Why Daylight Hours Change

Because Earth's axis is tilted, the circle of illumination (the boundary between day and night) does not pass through the poles. In summer, a location in the Southern Hemisphere spends more of its daily rotation on the sunlit side → more daylight hours. In winter, the opposite is true.

  • At the equator, day and night are always close to 12 hours each, year-round.
  • At the poles, there can be continuous day (midnight sun) or continuous night in extreme seasons.
  • In South Africa (mid-latitudes), daylight ranges from about 10 hours in winter to about 14 hours in summer.

Why Seasons Occur: Two Key Reasons

When a hemisphere is tilted toward the Sun, two things happen that make it warmer:

  • Higher sun angle: Sunlight strikes the ground more directly (less spread out), so energy per square metre is greater.
  • Longer days: More hours of daylight means more total energy received each day.

Time Zones

Because Earth is a sphere rotating 360° in 24 hours, it rotates 15° per hour. The world is divided into approximately 24 time zones, each roughly 15° of longitude wide. Countries to the east of you experience sunrise earlier. South Africa uses SAST (South Africa Standard Time) = UTC+2 — two hours ahead of Greenwich Mean Time.

South Africa does not use daylight saving time — our clocks stay the same all year round.
