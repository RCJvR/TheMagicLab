Biodiversity & Classification

Explore the incredible variety of life on Earth, understand why it matters, and learn how scientists organise all living things into groups.

What is Biodiversity?

Biodiversity (biological diversity) refers to the variety of life on Earth at all its levels. Scientists recognise three main levels of biodiversity:

  • Genetic diversity — variation in the genetic information (DNA) within a species. For example, not all lions look identical; individuals carry different gene variants.
  • Species diversity — the number and variety of different species in an area. A coral reef has far higher species diversity than a wheat field.
  • Ecosystem diversity — the variety of different ecosystems (habitats and communities) across the planet, from rainforests to deserts.
South Africa is one of the world's most biodiverse countries. The Cape Floristic Region (CFR) in the Western Cape is a global biodiversity hotspot containing roughly 9 000 plant species — about 70% of which are found nowhere else on Earth (endemic species). Kruger National Park supports the "Big Five" and thousands of other species across its 20 000 km² savanna ecosystem.

Why does Biodiversity Matter?

  • Ecosystem stability: More species means more resilient ecosystems — if one species is lost, others can fill its role.
  • Food security: Wild plants provide genetic material for breeding disease-resistant crops.
  • Medicine: Many medicines (aspirin, penicillin, anti-malarials) were originally derived from wild organisms.
  • Ecosystem services: Clean water, clean air, pollination, and soil fertility all depend on biodiversity.
  • Cultural and aesthetic value: Many South African communities and eco-tourism depend on natural biodiversity.

Classification of Living Things

Scientists use taxonomy to group and name organisms. The modern classification system was developed by Carl Linnaeus (18th century). Organisms are grouped by shared characteristics — today, mainly by evolutionary (genetic) relationships.

The classification hierarchy (from broadest to most specific):

Kingdom → Phylum → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species
Mnemonic: "King Philip Came Over For Good Soup"

Binomial Nomenclature

Every species is given a unique two-part scientific name (binomial nomenclature), following rules set by Linnaeus:

  • The first part is the Genus name — always capitalised.
  • The second part is the species name — always lowercase.
  • The full name is written in italics (or underlined when handwritten).
Examples:
Homo sapiens — modern humans
Panthera leo — lion
Protea cynaroides — King Protea (South Africa's national flower)
Acinonyx jubatus — cheetah

Dichotomous Keys

A dichotomous key is a tool scientists use to identify an unknown organism. It works by presenting a series of paired statements — at each step, you choose the one that applies, and follow the path until you reach the organism's identity.

"Dichotomous" means "split into two" — each question has exactly two choices (YES or NO, or Choice A vs Choice B). Dichotomous keys can be used to identify plants, animals, rocks, fungi, and more.
