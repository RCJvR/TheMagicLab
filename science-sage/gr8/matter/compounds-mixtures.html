Compounds & Mixtures

Understand how elements combine to form compounds, and how mixtures differ from pure substances.

Elements, Compounds, and Mixtures

All matter can be classified as either a pure substance or a mixture. Pure substances are made of only one type of particle and have fixed, definite properties. Mixtures contain two or more substances that are physically combined but not chemically joined.

Three key categories:
• Element — a pure substance made of only one type of atom (e.g. oxygen gas, O₂; iron, Fe)
• Compound — a pure substance made of two or more different elements chemically bonded together in a fixed ratio (e.g. water, H₂O; salt, NaCl)
• Mixture — two or more substances physically combined but not chemically bonded (e.g. air, seawater, soil)

Elements

An element is a pure substance that cannot be broken down into simpler substances by ordinary chemical means. There are 118 known elements, all listed on the periodic table. Elements exist as individual atoms (e.g. helium, He) or as molecules of the same type of atom (e.g. oxygen gas, O₂; hydrogen gas, H₂).

If a substance contains only ONE type of atom, it is an element. If it contains two or more DIFFERENT types of atom chemically bonded, it is a compound.

Compounds

A compound is formed when two or more different elements are chemically combined. The elements are joined by chemical bonds (ionic or covalent) in a fixed, definite ratio. Compounds have entirely different properties from the elements they are made of.

  • Water (H₂O): 2 hydrogen atoms + 1 oxygen atom. Both hydrogen and oxygen are flammable gases — yet water extinguishes fires!
  • Sodium chloride / table salt (NaCl): 1 sodium atom + 1 chlorine atom. Sodium is a dangerously reactive metal and chlorine is a toxic gas — yet together they make ordinary salt, essential for life.
  • Carbon dioxide (CO₂): 1 carbon atom + 2 oxygen atoms. Produced when fuels burn and during respiration.
  • Methane (CH₄): 1 carbon atom + 4 hydrogen atoms. Natural gas used for cooking and heating.
Key point: In a compound, the elements are present in a fixed ratio by mass. Water is always H₂O — never H₃O or HO. This is the Law of Definite Proportions. Compounds can only be separated by chemical reactions, not physical means.

Mixtures

A mixture contains two or more substances that are physically combined. The substances keep their own properties and can usually be separated by physical methods (e.g. filtration, evaporation).

  • The components are not chemically bonded.
  • The composition is variable — unlike a compound, the ratio can vary.
  • Each component retains its own chemical properties.
  • No energy is released or absorbed during mixing (usually).

Homogeneous vs Heterogeneous Mixtures

FeatureHomogeneous mixture (solution)Heterogeneous mixture
AppearanceUniform throughout — looks the same in every partNon-uniform — different parts look different
Components visible?No — components are not visible to the naked eyeOften yes — distinct parts can be seen
ExamplesSalt water, vinegar, air, bronze (alloy), soft drinkSand + water, soil, oil + water, salad, granite
SeparationUsually harder — requires evaporation or distillationEasier — filtration, sieving, or decanting

Air is a homogeneous mixture of nitrogen (~78%), oxygen (~21%), argon (~1%), and small amounts of other gases. It looks uniform, but can be separated into its component gases by fractional distillation of liquid air.

Physical vs Chemical Changes

Understanding how compounds and mixtures form requires knowing the difference between physical and chemical changes:

  • Physical change: No new substance is formed. The substance changes shape, size, or state (solid/liquid/gas), but its chemical identity stays the same. Examples: melting ice, dissolving sugar in water, tearing paper, boiling water. Physical changes are usually reversible.
  • Chemical change: A new substance with new properties is formed. The change involves breaking and forming chemical bonds. Examples: burning wood, rusting iron, cooking an egg, baking a cake. Chemical changes are usually irreversible.
Signs of a chemical change: colour change, gas produced (bubbling), temperature change, light produced, precipitate forms, new smell. Forming a compound = chemical change. Making a mixture = physical change.
