Chemical Equilibrium

Reversible Reactions

Many chemical reactions are reversible — they can proceed in both the forward direction (reactants → products) and the reverse direction (products → reactants). Reversible reactions are represented using a double arrow: ⇌

A + B ⇌ C + D

In a closed system, as the forward reaction produces C and D, those products can recombine in the reverse reaction to regenerate A and B. Over time, the system reaches a state where both reactions occur simultaneously.

Dynamic Equilibrium

Dynamic equilibrium is reached when the rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the reverse reaction. At this point:

  • The concentrations of all species remain constant (but not necessarily equal to each other)
  • Both the forward and reverse reactions are still occurring — it is dynamic, not static
  • Macroscopic properties (colour, pressure, concentration) are constant
  • A closed system is required — no reactants or products can escape
Common misconception: At equilibrium, concentrations of reactants and products are constant — but they are NOT necessarily equal. The position of equilibrium depends on the value of Kc. A large Kc means more product is present; a small Kc means more reactant remains.

The Equilibrium Constant Kc

For the general reaction: aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD, the equilibrium constant expression is:

Kc = [C]c[D]d / [A]a[B]b

Important rules:

  • Square brackets [ ] denote concentration in mol·L−1
  • Pure solids and pure liquids are NOT included in the Kc expression (their concentration is constant)
  • Gases and aqueous species ARE included
  • Kc depends only on temperature — it is constant at a given temperature regardless of starting concentrations
Kc valueInterpretation
Kc >> 1 (e.g. 10⁶)Products strongly favoured; reaction goes nearly to completion
Kc ≈ 1Neither products nor reactants strongly favoured; significant amounts of both at equilibrium
Kc << 1 (e.g. 10−⁶)Reactants strongly favoured; very little product formed at equilibrium

The Reaction Quotient Qc

The reaction quotient Qc has the same mathematical form as Kc, but uses current (non-equilibrium) concentrations. Comparing Qc to Kc tells us which direction the reaction will shift:

ComparisonDirection of shiftReason
Qc < KcForward (right) →Too many reactants; system needs more products to reach Kc
Qc = KcNo shift — at equilibriumSystem is already at equilibrium
Qc > KcReverse (left) ←Too many products; system needs more reactants to reach Kc

Le Chatelier’s Principle

Le Chatelier’s Principle: If a stress (change in conditions) is applied to a system at equilibrium, the system will shift in the direction that opposes the stress and re-establishes equilibrium.

Effect of Concentration

Stress appliedDirection of shiftEffect on Kc
Increase [reactant]Forward (right) →No change
Decrease [reactant]Reverse (left) ←No change
Increase [product]Reverse (left) ←No change
Decrease [product] (remove product)Forward (right) →No change

Effect of Pressure (Gas-phase reactions)

  • Increase pressure (decrease volume) → system shifts toward the side with fewer moles of gas
  • Decrease pressure (increase volume) → system shifts toward the side with more moles of gas
  • If the number of moles of gas is equal on both sides: no shift occurs
  • Adding an inert gas at constant volume: partial pressures of reactants/products unchanged → no shift

Effect of a Catalyst

  • A catalyst does NOT shift the equilibrium position
  • It increases the rate of both forward and reverse reactions equally
  • Equilibrium is reached faster, but the same concentrations and the same Kc result

Industrial Applications

Haber Process (synthesis of ammonia):

N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g)    ΔH = −92 kJ·mol−1
ConditionValue usedJustification
Pressure150–300 atm (high)Fewer moles of gas on product side (2 vs 4); high P favours NH₃ yield
Temperature400–500°C (moderate)Low T favours yield (exothermic) but rate too slow; compromise gives acceptable rate and yield
CatalystIron (Fe) with promotersReaches equilibrium faster; no effect on yield or Kc

Contact Process (synthesis of sulfur trioxide, step in H₂SO₄ manufacture):

2SO₂(g) + O₂(g) ⇌ 2SO₃(g)    ΔH = −196 kJ·mol−1

Similar reasoning: high pressure favours SO₃ (3 moles gas → 2 moles); moderate temperature (450°C); V₂O₅ catalyst.

Exam content (DBE and IEB) — ICE Tables

An ICE table (Initial, Change, Equilibrium) is used to systematically calculate equilibrium concentrations from initial conditions. Example for N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃:

[N₂][H₂][NH₃]
Initial (I)0.5001.5000
Change (C)−x−3x+2x
Equilibrium (E)0.500−x1.500−3x2x

Substitute equilibrium expressions into the Kc expression and solve for x. Then calculate all equilibrium concentrations.

Enrichment, beyond the 2025 DBE and IEB papers — Solubility Product Ksp

The solubility product Ksp is the equilibrium constant for the dissolution of a sparingly soluble ionic compound. For CaCO₃(s) ⇌ Ca²⁺(aq) + CO₃²−(aq):

Ksp = [Ca²⁺][CO₃²−]

The common ion effect: adding a common ion (e.g. Ca²⁺ from CaCl₂) shifts the dissolution equilibrium left, reducing solubility. If the ionic product Q > Ksp, precipitation occurs.

Predict first: A sealed syringe of brown NO₂ and colourless N₂O₄ is at equilibrium. If you push the plunger in, will the gas end up darker or paler, and does Kc change? Then test it: Choose 50 cm³ and watch the colour and both graphs, then try heating and adding a catalyst.

Question 1 — Writing the Kc Expression
Write the equilibrium constant expression Kc for each of the following reactions. State which species (if any) are excluded and why.

(a) N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g)
(b) 2SO₂(g) + O₂(g) ⇌ 2SO₃(g)
(c) CaCO₃(s) ⇌ CaO(s) + CO₂(g)
(d) Fe³⁺(aq) + SCN⁻(aq) ⇌ FeSCN²⁺(aq)

(6 marks)
Show memo
(a) Kc = [NH₃]²/([N₂][H₂]³)
(b) Kc = [SO₃]²/([SO₂]²[O₂])
(c) Kc = [CO₂] — CaCO₃ and CaO are solids; their concentrations are constant, so they are left out.
(d) Kc = [FeSCN²⁺]/([Fe³⁺][SCN⁻])
Nothing is excluded in (a), (b) and (d): all species are gases or aqueous.
Question 2 — Le Chatelier Predictions
Consider the reaction: CO(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ CH₄(g) + H₂O(g)    ΔH = −206 kJ

For each change below, state (i) the direction of shift and (ii) whether Kc increases, decreases, or stays the same:

(a) Increasing the concentration of CO
(b) Removing H₂O from the system
(c) Increasing the total pressure
(d) Increasing the temperature
(e) Adding an iron catalyst

(10 marks — 2 per part)
Show memo
(a) Forward (to use up CO); Kc unchanged
(b) Forward (to replace H₂O); Kc unchanged
(c) Forward — 4 mol gas → 2 mol gas, so the side with fewer moles is favoured; Kc unchanged
(d) Reverse — the forward reaction is exothermic, so the endothermic reverse reaction is favoured; Kc decreases
(e) No shift — a catalyst speeds up both reactions equally; Kc unchanged
Question 3 — Kc Calculation
At a certain temperature, the following equilibrium concentrations are measured for the reaction H₂(g) + I₂(g) ⇌ 2HI(g):

[H₂] = 0.40 mol·L−1    [I₂] = 0.20 mol·L−1    [HI] = 1.60 mol·L−1

(a) Write the Kc expression for this reaction.
(b) Calculate the value of Kc.
(c) Do the products or reactants dominate at equilibrium? Explain using your Kc value.
(4 marks)
Show memo
(a) Kc = [HI]²/([H₂][I₂])
(b) Kc = 1,60²/(0,40 × 0,20) = 32
(c) Products dominate: Kc > 1, so the numerator (products) is larger than the denominator.
Question 4 — Haber Process Justification
The Haber process: N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g)    ΔH = −92 kJ

(a) Explain why high pressure is used in the Haber process. In your answer, refer to the number of moles of gas on each side.
(b) The process uses a temperature of about 450°C. Explain the trade-off involved in choosing this temperature — why not use a very low temperature (for better yield) or a very high temperature (for faster rate)?
(c) Iron catalyst is used. Does the catalyst change the equilibrium position or the value of Kc? Explain.
(d) Unreacted N₂ and H₂ are recycled back into the reactor. Explain, using Le Chatelier’s Principle, why this improves the overall yield.
(8 marks)
Show memo
(a) 4 mol of gas on the left, 2 mol on the right. High pressure favours the reaction producing fewer moles of gas, so the forward reaction is favoured and more NH₃ forms (the rate also increases).
(b) A low temperature favours the exothermic forward reaction (higher yield) but the rate is too slow; a very high temperature gives a fast rate but favours the reverse reaction, so the yield is low. 450 °C gives an acceptable yield at an acceptable rate.
(c) No — the catalyst speeds up the forward and reverse reactions equally; equilibrium is only reached faster.
(d) NH₃ is removed (liquefied), so the forward reaction is favoured; the recycled N₂ and H₂ pass through again, so almost all of the reactants are eventually converted.
Question 5 — Qc vs Kc Prediction
At 700 K, Kc = 54.3 for the reaction H₂(g) + I₂(g) ⇌ 2HI(g).

A mixture is prepared with the following initial concentrations: [H₂] = 0.10 mol·L−1, [I₂] = 0.20 mol·L−1, [HI] = 0.40 mol·L−1.

(a) Calculate Qc for this mixture.
(b) Compare Qc to Kc. In which direction will the reaction proceed to reach equilibrium?
(c) As the reaction shifts, what happens to [HI]? Does it increase or decrease? Explain.
(4 marks)
Show memo
(a) Qc = [HI]²/([H₂][I₂]) = 0,40²/(0,10 × 0,20) = 8,0
(b) Qc < Kc, so the forward reaction is favoured until Qc = Kc.
(c) [HI] increases — more HI is formed by the forward reaction (and [H₂] and [I₂] decrease).

Exam-style question · (13 marks)
2,0 mol SO₂ and 1,5 mol O₂ are sealed in a 5 dm³ container and allowed to reach equilibrium at a certain temperature: 2SO₂(g) + O₂(g) ⇌ 2SO₃(g), ΔH < 0. At equilibrium the container holds 0,8 mol SO₃.
(a) Define chemical equilibrium. (2)
(b) Calculate the equilibrium constant, Kc, at this temperature. (6)
(c) The temperature is increased. State and explain how Kc changes. (3)
(d) The volume of the container is decreased at constant temperature. State how the amount of SO₃ changes and explain. (2)
Show memo
(a) A dynamic equilibrium in a closed system when the rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the reverse reaction ✓✓.
(b) Change: SO₃ +0,8 mol; SO₂ −0,8 mol; O₂ −0,4 mol ✓ (ratio). Equilibrium: SO₂ 1,2 mol; O₂ 1,1 mol; SO₃ 0,8 mol ✓. Concentrations (÷ 5): 0,24; 0,22; 0,16 mol·dm⁻³ ✓. Kc = [SO₃]²/([SO₂]²[O₂]) ✓ = 0,16²/(0,24² × 0,22) ✓ = 2,02 ✓
(c) Kc decreases ✓. The forward reaction is exothermic, so a higher temperature favours the endothermic reverse reaction ✓; [SO₃] falls and [SO₂] and [O₂] rise ✓.
(d) The amount of SO₃ increases ✓: higher pressure favours the reaction that produces fewer moles of gas (3 mol → 2 mol), the forward reaction ✓.
