The Photoelectric Effect

When light shines on a metal surface, electrons are ejected — but only if the frequency of light is at or above a minimum threshold frequency f₀. This cannot be explained by wave theory — it requires a particle model.

Einstein's explanation (1905): Light travels in discrete packets called photons, each with energy E = hf. An electron is only ejected if one photon has enough energy to overcome the binding energy of the metal (the work function).

W = hf₀     (work function — minimum energy to eject electron)
hf = W + Ek(max)
Ek(max) = hf − W = hf − hf₀
  • Frequency below f₀: No electrons ejected, no matter how intense the light
  • Frequency above f₀: Electrons ejected; increasing f → higher Ek(max)
  • Increasing intensity (more photons per second): more electrons ejected per second (higher current), but the kinetic energy of each electron does not increase
Evidence for particle nature: The photoelectric effect cannot be explained by waves. Waves would predict that any frequency (given enough time/intensity) could eject electrons. The existence of a sharp threshold frequency proves photons are real quanta of energy.

Emission and Absorption Spectra

Electrons in atoms exist at discrete energy levels. When an electron drops from a higher to a lower energy level, it emits a photon of a specific frequency: E = hf = E₂ − E₁.

  • Emission spectrum: Hot gas emits light at specific wavelengths → bright coloured lines on a dark background
  • Absorption spectrum: Cool gas absorbs the same wavelengths from white light passing through → dark lines on a continuous rainbow spectrum
  • Each element produces a unique spectral pattern → used to identify elements in distant stars (spectroscopy)

Wave-Particle Duality and Lasers

Light behaves as a wave (diffraction, interference) AND as a particle (photoelectric effect, Compton scattering). This is wave-particle duality. de Broglie extended this: all particles (electrons, protons) also have a wavelength:

λ = h / mv     (de Broglie wavelength)

Laser = Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation. Properties: monochromatic (one wavelength), coherent (all waves in phase), collimated (parallel beam). Uses: surgery, cutting, barcode scanners, fibre optics, CD/DVD readers.

Enrichment, beyond the 2025 DBE and IEB papers — Compton Scattering, de Broglie & Heisenberg

Compton scattering: When a high-energy photon (X-ray/gamma ray) collides with a free electron, the photon loses energy and its wavelength increases (Δλ = h/mc × (1 − cosθ)). This momentum transfer proves photons behave as particles with momentum p = h/λ = hf/c.

de Broglie wavelength calculations: An electron (m = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg) moving at v = 2 × 10⁶ m/s has λ = h/mv = 6.63×10⁻³⁴ / (9.11×10⁻³¹ × 2×10⁶) = 3.64 × 10⁻¹⁰ m — in the X-ray range, confirmed by electron diffraction experiments.

Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle (qualitative): It is impossible to simultaneously know both the exact position and exact momentum of a particle: Δx·Δp ≥ h/4π. This is not a measurement error — it is a fundamental property of quantum systems. The more precisely we know position, the less precisely we can know momentum, and vice versa.

Predict first: Above the threshold frequency, does brighter light give more electrons or faster electrons? Then test it: Change the intensity, then the frequency, and compare the readings.

Question 5
A hot gas of hydrogen emits light at specific wavelengths (an emission spectrum). When cool hydrogen gas is placed in front of a white light source, an absorption spectrum is observed. (a) Explain why emission and absorption spectra occur at the same wavelengths. (b) How do scientists use spectra to identify elements in distant stars? (c) What does the spacing between spectral lines tell us about the energy levels in an atom?
Show memo
(a) An electron can only move between fixed energy levels. It absorbs a photon whose energy exactly equals the gap between two levels, and emits a photon of the same energy when it falls back, so absorption and emission lines occur at the same wavelengths.
(b) The dark absorption lines in a star’s spectrum are compared with the known line patterns (‘fingerprints’) of elements measured in the laboratory.
(c) Each line corresponds to the energy difference between two levels; the lines get closer together at shorter wavelengths, showing that the energy levels get closer together further from the nucleus.
