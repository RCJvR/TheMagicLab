Biodiversity of Plants

Plants are grouped into four divisions that reveal a clear evolutionary trend: decreasing dependence on water for reproduction. Explore bryophytes, pteridophytes, gymnosperms, and angiosperms.

Four divisions of the plant kingdom

Plants (Kingdom Plantae) are grouped into four major divisions. Moving from bryophytes through to angiosperms, there's a clear evolutionary trend: decreasing dependence on water for reproduction, alongside increasing structural complexity.

DivisionKey featuresReproduces via
BryophytesSimple moss plants; no vascular tissue, no true leaves or rootsSpores — needs water for reproduction
PteridophytesRhizome and fronds (leaves) bearing sori (spore clusters); has vascular tissueSpores — still needs water
GymnospermsTrue leaves (often needles) and roots; vascular tissue; conesSeeds — not enclosed in fruit
AngiospermsTrue leaves and roots; vascular tissue; flowersSeeds — enclosed in fruit

The big structural leap: vascular tissue

Bryophytes lack vascular tissue (xylem and phloem), which limits their size and ties them to damp habitats. Pteridophytes, gymnosperms, and angiosperms all have vascular tissue, allowing water and nutrient transport over longer distances — enabling larger plants that can grow further from water sources.

Seeds versus spores

Gymnosperms and angiosperms produce seeds rather than spores. A seed contains a protected embryo with a food store, giving it a major survival advantage over a spore. The key difference between the two seed-producing groups is that angiosperm seeds are enclosed in a fruit, while gymnosperm seeds (e.g. in a pine cone) are not.
