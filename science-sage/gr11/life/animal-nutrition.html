Animal Nutrition

From ingestion to egestion: follow food through the mammalian digestive system, and see how dentition reveals whether an animal is a herbivore, carnivore, or omnivore.

Five stages of processing food

ProcessDescription
IngestionTaking food into the mouth
DigestionBreaking food down — mechanically (chewing, peristalsis) and chemically (enzymes)
AbsorptionDigested food molecules pass into the blood, mainly across the wall of the small intestine
AssimilationAbsorbed nutrients are incorporated into and used by body cells (largely via the liver)
EgestionRemoval of undigested waste from the body as faeces

Mechanical and chemical digestion

Mechanical digestion physically breaks food into smaller pieces — chewing, and peristalsis (rhythmic muscle contractions that push food along the gut). Chemical digestion uses enzymes to break large molecules into small, absorbable ones: carbohydrases break down carbohydrates, proteases break down proteins, and lipases break down fats.

Absorption and the role of the liver

The small intestine is the main site of absorption — its inner wall is covered in tiny finger-like projections called villi, which greatly increase surface area for absorbing nutrients. Absorbed food travels via the hepatic portal system to the liver, which assimilates glucose and amino acids, and breaks down (deaminates) excess amino acids as well as alcohol and other drugs.

Dentition reflects diet

A mammal's teeth are adapted to its diet. Compare herbivores, carnivores, and omnivores in the Explore tab.

Diet-related health: malnutrition can result from an unbalanced diet — too little (e.g. marasmus, kwashiorkor) or an imbalance leading to conditions like obesity, heart disease, or diabetes. Diet also varies for cultural, religious, and personal reasons (e.g. halaal, kosher, vegan, vegetarian).
