Classification of Micro-organisms

Building on Grade 9 and 10: explore bacteria, protista, fungi, and viruses in more depth — their structure, their role in maintaining balance in the environment, and the diseases some of them cause.

An enormous, mostly invisible world

Life exists in a huge variety of forms living in different niches — from macroscopic plants and animals down to microscopic organisms. This lesson focuses on four groups of micro-organisms: bacteria, protista, fungi, and viruses, building on the classification basics from Grade 10.

Micro-organisms play a vital role in ecosystems

Far from being purely harmful, most micro-organisms help maintain balance in the environment and the wider web of life:

  • Nitrogen-fixing bacteria live in symbiotic relationships in the root nodules of certain plants, converting atmospheric nitrogen into a usable form.
  • E. coli bacteria live in the human intestine, assisting with digestion in a mutually beneficial relationship.
  • Many bacteria and fungi act as decomposers, recycling nutrients back into ecosystems.

Micro-organisms that cause disease

Some micro-organisms are pathogens — they cause disease in plants, animals, and humans:

GroupExample diseases
FungiRusts (in plants), thrush, ringworm, athlete's foot
ProtistaMalaria
BacteriaBlight (in plants), cholera, tuberculosis, anthrax
VirusesRabies, HIV/AIDS, influenza

Immunity and disease control

Plants and animals have immune responses that help defend against infecting micro-organisms. Vaccinations work by preparing the immune system to recognise and fight a specific pathogen before a real infection occurs. Drugs, such as antibiotics, are also used to treat infections caused by bacteria.

Micro-organisms working for us

Humans have long put micro-organisms to work:

  • Medicine production — e.g. insulin and antibiotics are produced using micro-organisms.
  • Traditional food technology — e.g. yeast (a fungus) is used to produce beer, wine, and bread; bacteria are used to produce cheese.
