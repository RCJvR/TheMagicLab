Freehand Drawing

Sketching technique, proportion, and line control without instruments — the basic hand movements needed to draw proportional single, multi-view and pictorial drawings on plain paper.

Freehand vs. instrument drawing

Not every drawing starts with a ruler and compass. Freehand drawing — sketching without instruments — is how engineers and designers quickly explore ideas before committing to an accurate instrument drawing. It's faster, but it still isn't careless: freehand work follows the exact same SANS 0111/0143 line-type conventions as instrument drawing.

Type A (thick) = outlines. Type B (medium) = dimensions & writing. Type C (thin) = construction & guidelines — even freehand.

Freehand lettering

All writing on an EGD drawing — titles, dimensions, notes — follows the SANS 0111/0143 single-stroke lettering style: plain, upright (or uniformly sloped) capital letters and numerals, every one the same height, with even spacing between letters and words.

  • Sketch light Type C guidelines first — a cap-height line and a baseline — before writing a single letter.
  • Keep every letter between the two guidelines; never let a letter float above the cap line or sink below the baseline.
  • Build each letter from a small number of straight strokes and simple arcs — avoid decorative flourishes.

Hand movement & line control

Confident freehand lines come from how you move, not just how carefully you aim:

  • Rest your whole forearm on the table — don't draw with just your fingers.
  • Mark your start and end points first, then draw the line in one long, smooth stroke rather than several short, scratchy ones.
  • Look at the target point you are drawing towards, not at your pencil tip — this guides your hand to travel straight, instead of over-correcting for tiny wobbles.
  • Draw horizontal lines left-to-right and vertical lines top-to-bottom (for right-handers); rotate the paper if a line runs at an awkward angle.

Sketching circles freehand — the box method

An accurate freehand circle almost always starts with a guideline, not a single blind stroke:

  • Lightly sketch a bounding square (Type C) around where the circle will go.
  • Mark the midpoint of each of the four sides — these four points all lie exactly on the circle.
  • Connect the four midpoints with four short freehand arcs, one per side of the square.
Why it works: a circle's radius touches the midpoint of a tangent square on every side, so the box gives you four guaranteed points to arc between — far more accurate than guessing a curve freehand with no guide at all.

Blocking-in & proportion

Blocking-in is the single most important freehand skill CAPS assesses at Grade 10: before you sketch any detail, lightly rough in an overall bounding box (Type C) that captures the object's correct proportions — its width-to-height ratio and the position of its major features. Only once the blocking guidelines are in place do you sketch the real outline over them in a darker Type A line.

Block first (light Type C) → subdivide for major features → outline last (dark Type A).

Enlarging a freehand drawing

A common Grade 10 task is to enlarge a small reference drawing freehand by an exact ratio, e.g. 2:1. The blocking-in method makes this manageable: scale the bounding block itself by the same ratio in both directions (double both width and height for a 2:1 enlargement), subdivide the enlarged block the same way you subdivided the original, and then sketch the enlarged outline using those guides. This keeps every proportion identical to the original — just bigger.

CAPS Grade 10 requirement: your enlarged freehand drawing must show clear evidence of the blocking guidelines used to establish correct proportions — don't erase them completely before handing in your practical work.

Sketching on a raster (square) grid

CAPS specifically allows freehand sketching "on plain paper and/or grid sheets" — and a plain square grid is one of the most effective proportion aids there is. Instead of guessing measurements, describe the object in grid squares: "the body is 6 squares wide and 3 squares tall, with a hole 2 squares in from the left." Sketching multi-view (front and side view) drawings on a raster grid is how you'll practise reading and reproducing proportions before moving on to instrument-drawn orthographic projection.

Count squares, don't estimate distances — the grid does the proportion-checking for you.

Sketching on an isometric (30°) grid

A pictorial (3D-look) freehand sketch is far easier to keep consistent on an isometric grid: a pattern of lines running vertically, and at 30° to the horizontal in both directions. These three directions are the three isometric axes. As long as every edge of your sketch runs along one of these three grid directions, your pictorial sketch will look proportioned and consistent — even freehand, with no instruments at all.
