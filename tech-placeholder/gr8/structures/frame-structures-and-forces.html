Frame Structures & Forces

Roof trusses, bridges and pylons all rely on structural members working together. Learn how forces act on each member, and why triangulation makes structures stiff.

What is a frame structure?

A frame structure is built from a skeleton of separate structural members (beams, poles, struts) joined together, rather than a single solid mass. Roof trusses, electrical pylons, and bicycle frames are all frame structures — they use far less material than a solid structure while still carrying heavy loads.

Roof truss members: a king post truss has a tie beam (the horizontal base), rafters (the sloping top members), a king post (the central vertical member), and struts (diagonal supports). A queen post truss uses two vertical posts instead of one, for wider spans.

Forces on structural members

Every member in a frame structure experiences one or more of these forces:

  • Tension — a pulling/stretching force that tries to lengthen the member.
  • Compression — a pushing/squeezing force that tries to shorten the member.
  • Torsion — a twisting force.
  • Shear — two forces acting in opposite directions, sliding past each other (like scissors cutting paper).
Ties are in tension. Struts are in compression. That's why they're named differently!

Case study: electrical pylons

Pylon designs vary widely, but they all solve the same problem: holding heavy power lines high above the ground, safely, in wind. Look closely at any pylon and you'll see a lattice of small triangles rather than large open squares. This is triangulation — a triangle is the only shape that cannot change shape without one of its sides changing length, so triangulated frames resist twisting and bending far better than a frame of squares. The diagonal cross-bracing members convert what would be a wobbly rectangle into two rigid triangles.

Why structures fail

There are three main ways a structure can fail:

  • Fracture — a member snaps because it isn't strong enough for the force on it.
  • Bending / buckling — a member flexes or buckles because it isn't stiff (rigid) enough.
  • Toppling over — the whole structure falls over because it isn't stable (it's too top-heavy or has too narrow a base).
Strength vs stiffness vs stability are three different properties — a member can be strong enough not to snap, yet not stiff enough to stop it bending, on a structure that is still stable overall.

Communicating with graphics

Before anything gets built, an idea has to be worked out and shown to other people — a client, a builder, an assessor. That's the purpose of graphics: technical drawing lets you develop your own idea clearly in your head, and communicate that exact idea to someone else without any confusion.

Drawing conventions: line types

Technical drawings use different line types so anyone reading them instantly knows what each line means:

  • Outlines — thick, dark, solid lines showing the visible edges of the object.
  • Construction lines — thin, feint (light) lines used to set out a drawing accurately. They guide your work but are not part of the final visible outline.
  • Hidden detail — dashed lines showing an edge that exists but is hidden behind other material in that view.
  • Centre lines — a chain of alternating long and short dashes (dash-dot) marking the centre of a symmetrical feature.

Drawings are also scaled up or down from real life so they fit on the page, and dimensioned — every measurement is written on the drawing in millimetres, so the exact size is never in doubt.

Outline = thick and solid. Construction = thin and feint. Hidden detail = dashed. Centre line = dash-dot-dash.

Working drawing techniques

A single-view flat 2D drawing shows one face of an object flat-on, drawn to scale with correct line types and dimensions — enough detail for someone to make a simple, flat part.

For a more realistic 3D view, use isometric drawing: sketch on top of an isometric grid, where all lines run vertically or at 30° to the horizontal in two directions. Because the grid keeps every angle consistent, you can produce an accurate-looking 3D sketch quickly, without needing drawing instruments.

Artistic drawing: perspective, texture & shading

For the most realistic, eye-catching impression of a design, technologists use double vanishing point perspective: two vanishing points are marked on a horizon line, and every edge of the object that runs "into the distance" is drawn heading toward one of these two points. This mimics the way parallel lines appear to converge as they get further from your eye — think of looking down a long straight road.

Artistic drawings usually start as a quick freehand sketch, using only a pencil, a ruler for straight reference lines, and blank paper — no measuring instruments needed at this stage. The sketch is then enhanced to look more realistic by adding colour, texture and shading (darker tones on surfaces facing away from the light) and shadows cast onto the ground or nearby surfaces.

Isometric = fast, accurate 3D idea sketch using a grid. Perspective = the most realistic-looking artistic impression, using vanishing points.
