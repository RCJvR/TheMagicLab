Circuits & Electrochemical Cells

Input, output and control devices, correct connections versus short circuits, and how to draw a circuit using accepted electrical symbols.

Input, output and control devices

Every electrical circuit is a system: something provides energy, something uses it, and something controls the flow. Components are grouped by their role in the circuit:

  • Input devices — supply electrical energy into the circuit: an electrochemical cell (battery), a generator, or a solar (photovoltaic) panel.
  • Output devices — convert electrical energy into another useful form: a resistor (limits current), lamp (light), heater (heat), buzzer (sound), or motor (movement).
  • Control devices — manage when and how current flows: switches of all kinds.
Some devices wear more than one hat! A motor is usually an output device, but spun by hand it can act as a generator (input). A resistor can act as an output device (limiting current) or as part of a sensing/control circuit. Always classify a device by the job it is doing in that specific circuit.

Correct connections and short circuits

For a circuit to work, current must flow in one continuous, unbroken loop from the cell, through the components, and back to the cell. This is called a closed circuit. If the loop is broken anywhere (a wire disconnected, a switch open), it is an open circuit and no current flows.

A short circuit happens when current finds an unintended low-resistance path that bypasses the output device — for example, if the two terminals of a lamp are connected directly to each other by a stray wire. Almost all the current rushes through this shortcut instead of through the lamp, so the lamp stays dark, the wires overheat, and the cell drains very quickly. Short circuits are dangerous and can start fires.

Correct circuit: one continuous loop through every component. Short circuit: a "shortcut" path that skips the output device.

Electrical component symbols

Circuits are drawn using standard, internationally accepted symbols so that anyone, anywhere, can read the diagram the same way. You must be able to recognise and draw these symbols:

ComponentRoleSymbol description
CellInputOne long and one short parallel line (long = positive)
Battery (multiple cells)InputTwo or more cell symbols in a row
LampOutputA circle with an X or cross inside it
ResistorOutputA rectangle (or zig-zag line in older diagrams)
SwitchControlA break in the line with a short diagonal "lever" line
BuzzerOutputA circle or semicircle labelled with a wavy sound line
MotorOutputA circle with an "M" inside it

Connecting wires are drawn as straight lines, and every component is placed in the wire loop — never floating off to the side. Junctions where wires join are shown with a solid dot.

Setting up circuits with a range of components

When building a real circuit, always: (1) connect components in a single closed loop, (2) check the cell's positive and negative terminals are the right way round for polarised components, (3) place the switch so it can safely cut off the whole circuit, and (4) test with a low-cost output device (like a lamp) before adding more components. Once built, the physical circuit should always be redrawn as a symbol diagram — this is how technologists record and communicate a design.
