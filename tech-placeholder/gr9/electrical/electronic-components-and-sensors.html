Electronic Components & Sensors

Switches, diodes, LEDs, transistors and sensors are the building blocks of every electronic device. Learn how each one behaves, and how they combine into working alarm circuits.

Manual switches

A switch is a component controlled directly by the user to open or close a circuit. Different switch types are named by how many separate circuits ("poles") and positions ("throws") they control:

SwitchMeaningTypical use
Push buttonOnly makes contact while it is held downDoorbells, keypads
SPSTSingle-pole-single-throw — one circuit, on/off onlySimple lamp switch
SPDTSingle-pole-double-throw — one circuit, switches between two connectionsStairway light switch (two-way)
DPDTDouble-pole-double-throw — two separate circuits switched together, each with two positionsReversing a motor's direction

Diodes and LEDs

A diode is a component that allows current to flow in one direction only. If it is connected the wrong way round, no current flows at all — this protects circuits from a wrongly connected battery.

A LED (light-emitting diode) works the same way — current can only flow through it in one direction — but a LED also gives off light when current flows. Because of this, LEDs are very often used as a small, low-power indicator to show that a circuit is switched "ON".

Remember: both diodes and LEDs are one-direction-only components — connect them backwards and the circuit simply won't work.

Transistors

At this level we only work with the NPN transistor. A transistor is a small semiconductor device that can do two important jobs:

  • Act as an electronic switch — a tiny current into its base pin can switch a much larger current on or off between its other two pins.
  • Amplify a small current — for example, the very small current produced by a sensor — into a larger current strong enough to drive a buzzer, lamp or motor.

This is why transistors are essential in sensor-based circuits: a sensor alone often cannot supply enough current to sound a buzzer directly, but it can supply enough to switch a transistor, which then drives the buzzer.

Sensors — important input devices

Sensors are input devices that change their electrical behaviour in response to something in the environment, so a circuit can react automatically.

SensorHow it behaves
LDR (light dependent resistor)Resistance decreases as light increases: dark = high resistance, bright light = low resistance
+t thermistorResistance increases as temperature increases
-t thermistorResistance decreases as temperature increases
Touch / moisture detectorA "wet" finger bridges two contacts, completing the circuit and signalling a touch
CapacitorStores electrical energy, then releases it — used for smoothing or timing
LDR vs thermistor: both are resistors whose resistance changes with a condition in their environment (light or temperature) rather than a fixed value — that's what makes them useful as sensors instead of ordinary resistors.

Putting it together: simple electronic circuits

CAPS Grade 9 requires you to be able to draw and assemble these standard circuits:

  • LED + 470Ω resistor + switch + 4,5V battery — the simplest indicator circuit; the resistor protects the LED from too much current.
  • LDR + buzzer + 3V battery — a light-triggered alarm: as light falls on the LDR its resistance drops, allowing enough current to sound the buzzer (or vice versa, depending on wiring) — useful as a "light left on" or intruder alert.
  • NPN transistor + buzzer + thermistor + variable resistor + 1kΩ resistor + 6V battery — a temperature-triggered alarm: the thermistor's changing resistance controls the small base current into the transistor, which then switches the larger buzzer current on when a set temperature is reached. The variable resistor sets the trigger threshold.
  • 6V battery + LED + 470Ω resistor + 1000μF capacitor + switch — shows how a capacitor stores charge and keeps the LED glowing briefly even after the switch is opened.
Why a resistor with an LED? An LED has very little resistance of its own, so without a resistor in series, too much current would flow and destroy it. The resistor limits the current to a safe level.
