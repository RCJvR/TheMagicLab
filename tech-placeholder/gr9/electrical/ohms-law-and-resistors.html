Ohm's Law & Resistor Colour Codes

Voltage, current and resistance are linked by one simple equation. Learn the three forms of Ohm's Law, and how to read a resistor's value from its coloured bands.

Revision: component symbols

Before tackling Ohm's Law, make sure you can recognise these circuit basics:

  • Cells in series add their voltages together (two 1.5 V cells in series give 3 V); cells in parallel keep the same voltage but can supply current for longer.
  • Lamps in series share the circuit's voltage and all go out if one fails (one path for current); lamps in parallel each get the full voltage and keep working if one fails (multiple paths for current).
  • Switches in series behave like AND logic — every switch must be closed for current to flow. Switches in parallel behave like OR logic — current flows if any one switch is closed.
  • Conventional current is defined as flowing from the positive terminal of a cell, through the circuit, to the negative terminal.

Ohm's Law — the relationship

Ohm's Law describes how voltage, current and resistance relate in a circuit: if the resistance stays constant, increasing the voltage across a component causes the current through it to increase too. Voltage is the "push"; resistance is the "opposition"; current is the resulting flow.

In an experiment connecting one, then two, then three cells to the same 20 Ω resistor, the ammeter reading rises in step with the voltmeter reading — plotting these results gives a straight line through the origin, showing current is directly proportional to voltage when resistance is constant.

The three forms of the equation

Ohm's Law can be rearranged to solve for whichever quantity is unknown:

EquationUse when you knowSolves for
R = V ÷ IVoltage and currentResistance (Ω)
V = I × RCurrent and resistanceVoltage (V)
I = V ÷ RVoltage and resistanceCurrent (A)
R = resistance in ohms (Ω) · V = potential difference in volts (V) · I = current strength in amperes (A)

Resistor colour codes

Resistors control how much current flows in a circuit. Their resistance value must be marked somehow:

  • Low-value resistors often simply have their resistance value printed on them in numbers.
  • Higher-value resistors are marked with coloured bands instead, since printed text would be too small to read reliably.

On a standard four-band resistor: the first three bands combine to give the resistance value in ohms (the first two are significant digits, the third is a multiplier), and the fourth band is a tolerance rating — how accurate the resistor is, given as a percentage (e.g. gold = ±5%, silver = ±10%).

Colour-to-digit key (common mnemonic order): Black = 0, Brown = 1, Red = 2, Orange = 3, Yellow = 4, Green = 5, Blue = 6, Violet = 7, Grey = 8, White = 9.
