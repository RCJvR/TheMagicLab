Advanced Gear Systems

Beyond simple spur gears — bevel gears change the axis of rotation, rack-and-pinion converts rotary motion to linear motion, and worm gears trade speed for enormous force.

Revision: spur gears

Spur gears are the simplest gear type — flat, toothed wheels mounted on parallel axles that mesh together and rotate on the same plane.

  • Equal-size spur gears counter-rotate at the same speed with the same force — one turns clockwise, the meshed gear turns anticlockwise.
  • Unequal-size spur gears also counter-rotate, but the smaller gear spins faster with less force, while the larger gear spins slower with more force (a velocity/force trade-off — you can never gain both at once).
  • An idler gear is a third gear placed between two others purely to make the final gear rotate in the same direction as the first (synchronising direction) — it does not change speed or force, since its size doesn't affect the overall ratio between the driver and final gear.
Smaller driven gear = faster, weaker. Larger driven gear = slower, stronger.

Bevel gears

Bevel gears have angled (conical) teeth, which allow them to mesh at an angle — most commonly changing the axis of rotation by 90°.

  • Equal-size bevel gears change the direction of the drive by 90° but keep the same speed and force.
  • Unequal-size bevel gears also change the axis of rotation by 90°, and — just like spur gears — the smaller gear spins faster with less force while the larger gear spins slower with more force.
Real-world example: the differential in a car uses bevel gears to transfer rotation from the engine's driveshaft (spinning front-to-back) to the wheel axles (spinning side-to-side) — a 90° change in axis.

Rack-and-pinion gear systems

A rack-and-pinion system pairs a circular gear (the pinion) with a flat toothed bar (the rack). As the pinion rotates, its teeth push against the rack's teeth, converting rotary motion into linear (straight-line) motion — or the reverse.

Rack-and-pinion: rotary motion ⇄ linear motion.

You'll find rack-and-pinion systems in automatic sliding gates (a motor turns a pinion that drives the gate along a fixed rack) and in a car's steering rack (turning the steering wheel rotates a pinion that slides the rack left or right, turning the front wheels).

Worm gear systems

A worm gear system uses a screw-like "worm" that meshes with a toothed wheel (the worm wheel). Each full turn of the worm advances the worm wheel by only one tooth, so a worm gear produces a huge reduction in speed and, correspondingly, a huge increase in force.

Worm gears are used in heavy machinery, garage door openers, and guitar tuning pegs — anywhere a small motor (or hand) needs to generate a large turning force slowly. A useful safety feature is that most worm gear systems are self-locking: the worm wheel cannot easily turn the worm backwards, so the mechanism holds its position without a separate brake.

Integrated systems: combining mechanical, electrical and hydraulic/pneumatic

Real machines are rarely just one type of system working alone — most useful devices integrate two or more sub-systems, each handling the part it does best. A mechanical system can multiply force or change motion, but something has to switch it on and off, and something has to supply the driving force in the first place.

Worked example — an electric car jack:
INPUT: the user presses an electrical switch, sending current to a small motor → PROCESS: the motor's fast, weak rotation drives a mechanical worm gear, which trades speed for a huge increase in force (and self-locks, so the jack can't slip back down) → OUTPUT: a screw thread turns and lifts the car — far more force than the small motor alone could ever produce.

A machine's control device — the part that starts, stops or holds a system in place — can itself be mechanical (a cleat that jams a rope, a ratchet and pawl that allows movement one way only) or electrical (a simple switch). Choosing the right control device is part of designing the overall system, not just an afterthought.

Design tip: when a scenario asks for a device combining two or more of mechanical / electrical / hydraulic / pneumatic systems, sketch it as input → process → output first — it makes it obvious which sub-system does which job.
