Pulleys & Mechanical Control Systems

Pulleys let us redirect or multiply a pulling force. Control systems like ratchets, brakes and cleats let us control motion — allowing it, stopping it, or locking it in place.

The single fixed pulley

A single fixed pulley is a wheel with a grooved rim, mounted in a fixed position (for example, bolted to a beam), with a rope running over it. Pulling down on one end of the rope lifts the load on the other end.

A fixed pulley does not multiply force — the effort you apply equals the load you lift. Its only job is to change the direction of the pulling force, which is why a flagpole lets you raise a flag by pulling downward instead of climbing up.

Single fixed pulley: Mechanical Advantage (MA) = 1. Direction changes, force does not.
Single fixed pulley — one rope section supports the load

The single moveable pulley

A single moveable pulley is attached directly to the load, so the pulley itself moves as the load is lifted. One end of the rope is fixed (often to the same beam or frame), the rope loops under the moveable pulley, and you pull on the free end.

Because the load is now supported by two rope sections instead of one, you only need to pull with about half the force to lift it — giving MA > 1. The trade-off is that you must pull the rope twice as far to raise the load the same distance.

Single moveable pulley: MA = 2 (approximately). Effort ≈ half the load, but rope moves twice as far.
Single moveable pulley — two rope sections support the load

Pulley block systems (block and tackle)

A block and tackle combines several fixed and moveable pulleys (mounted in "blocks") with one continuous rope threaded through them. The more load-bearing rope sections supporting the moveable block, the greater the mechanical advantage — each extra rope section shares the load between more strands.

Rule of thumb: Mechanical Advantage ≈ the number of rope sections directly supporting the moveable pulley block. Two supporting ropes give MA ≈ 2; three supporting ropes give MA ≈ 3, and so on. This is how cranes and engine hoists lift loads far heavier than a person could pull directly.
2-pulley block and tackle — four rope sections support the moveable block

Mechanical control systems

A control system doesn't create motion — it manages it: allowing movement in only one direction, slowing it down, or locking it in place.

  • Ratchet and pawl — a toothed wheel (ratchet) engages a spring-loaded arm (pawl) that slots between the teeth. The wheel can turn freely in one direction, but the pawl catches the teeth and blocks rotation the other way. Used in ratchet spanners, fishing reels, and hand winches to stop back-motion.
  • Disc brake — a flat metal disc (rotor) spins with the wheel; squeezing brake pads against both faces of the disc using friction slows or stops the rotation. Common on cars, motorbikes, and bicycles.
  • Bicycle brake — rubber brake blocks are squeezed against the wheel rim (rim brake) by a hand lever and cable, using friction to slow the bicycle. Some bicycles instead use a small disc brake at the hub.
  • Cleat — a fitting (often on a boat) that a rope is wound around in a figure-of-eight pattern. Friction between the rope and the cleat's horns wedges the rope in place, securing it under load without needing a knot.
Common thread: ratchets control direction, brakes and cleats control motion using friction — pressing one surface against another to convert movement into heat and hold or slow the system.
